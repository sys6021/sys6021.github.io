Basic Course Information

Course Description:

How can we use data to understand complex systems, evaluate relationships, make predictions, and support better decisions? How do we determine which patterns in data are meaningful, which models are appropriate, and how much confidence we should place in their results?

This course introduces the foundations of statistical modeling and machine learning. We examine the full modeling process: exploring and preparing data, formulating questions and hypotheses, constructing models, evaluating assumptions and performance, comparing alternative models, and translating results into meaningful conclusions and actionable recommendations.

Topics include data visualization and exploration, statistical inference, linear and generalized linear models, analysis of variance and covariance, model diagnostics and selection, missing data, dimensionality reduction, classification, and prediction. The course considers both deductive approaches, in which models are motivated by hypotheses and knowledge about a system, and inductive approaches, in which models and patterns are learned from data. This progression provides a bridge from classical statistical modeling to modern machine learning.

Students use R to analyze real datasets, build and evaluate models, and communicate results through reproducible analyses. The course provides a foundation for more advanced work in data mining and data-driven systems engineering.

Learning Objectives:
  • Apply statistical modeling to understand complex systems and support evidence-informed engineering decisions
  • Explore, visualize, and assess data to identify patterns, limitations, and potential sources of bias
  • Formulate meaningful, testable questions and hypotheses from real-world problems and associated data
  • Select and apply appropriate modeling techniques for inference, prediction, and classification
  • Build and interpret linear and generalized linear models using R
  • Evaluate model assumptions, diagnostics, uncertainty, and performance
  • Identify and address issues including missing data, outliers, influential observations, overfitting, and model generalization
  • Compare alternative models and explain tradeoffs between explanatory and predictive modeling approaches
  • Apply machine learning methods and evaluate their performance and generalization ability
  • Develop actionable recommendations supported by statistical evidence while clearly communicating uncertainty and limitations
  • Communicate data analyses, modeling decisions, and results through reproducible analyses and technical reports
Prerequisites:

It is recommended that students have a basic command of linear algebra, calculus, probability, statistics and programming experience.

Course Resources

There is no required textbook for this course. The following open-access texts and references will support course topics in statistical modeling, machine learning, and R.

  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning by Gareth James, Daniela Witten, Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, and Jonathan Taylor. An accessible introduction to statistical modeling, prediction, classification, and machine learning using R.
  • The Elements of Statistical Learning by Trevor Hastie, Robert Tibshirani, and Jerome Friedman. A more advanced reference on statistical learning and machine learning.
  • Linear Models with R by Julian Faraway. A reference for linear models, inference, diagnostics, and modeling in R.
  • Applied Linear Statistical Models by Michael Kutner, Christopher Nachtsheim, John Neter, and William Li. A comprehensive reference for regression, analysis of variance, and related statistical modeling methods.
  • R for Data Science (2e) by Hadley Wickham, Mine Çetinkaya-Rundel, and Garrett Grolemund. A practical reference for working with, visualizing, transforming, and communicating data using R.

We will use R and RStudio throughout the course for data analysis, visualization, statistical modeling, and machine learning.

The Use of Generative AI:

Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, and other similar technologies are increasingly part of modern computational practice. Learning how to use these tools effectively, critically, and responsibly is an important part of this course. When permitted, you are encouraged to use generative AI as a learning, co-design, and programming partner rather than simply as a tool for producing answers.

Appropriate uses of generative AI may include brainstorming and co-designing solutions, exploring alternative analytical or computational approaches, explaining concepts, generating examples, writing or modifying code, debugging and interpreting error messages, reviewing or critiquing code and analyses, and assisting with documentation. The specific expectations for generative AI use may vary by assignment and will be communicated when necessary.

You remain responsible for all work that you submit. You should understand the code, analyses, methodological choices, and conclusions in your work and be able to explain and defend them. AI-generated output can be incorrect, incomplete, biased, or inappropriate for the problem at hand. You are responsible for critically evaluating and verifying any AI-generated code, information, or recommendations before incorporating them into your work. Generative AI should support your learning and decision-making, not replace them.

You are also responsible for the accuracy and integrity of all references and sources in your work. Generative AI tools may fabricate citations, authors, titles, quotations, URLs, or other source information. You must personally verify that every source you cite exists, that the citation information is accurate, and that the source actually supports the claim for which it is cited. Do not cite a source solely because a generative AI tool provided or summarized it without consulting and verifying the original source.

As the author of submitted work, you retain responsibility for its accuracy, originality, attribution, and integrity regardless of whether generative AI was used in its development. The use of generative AI does not transfer responsibility for errors, fabricated information or references, plagiarism, unsupported claims, or other problems to the AI tool.

Co-design does not mean outsourcing the work. You may use generative AI to help develop ideas, code, and alternative approaches, but you are responsible for making the final design and analytical decisions. Submitting AI-generated work that you do not understand, cannot explain, or have not critically evaluated is inconsistent with the expectations of this course.

Meaningful use of generative AI on submitted work must be acknowledged and documented. When generative AI contributes to an assignment or project, briefly identify the tool used and describe how it contributed to your work (for example, brainstorming an approach, co-designing a solution, debugging code, generating an initial implementation, or reviewing an analysis). You do not need to document incidental uses that do not contribute to submitted work unless otherwise specified.

Generative AI is not permitted on exams. Exams are designed to assess your independent understanding of the programming, computational, and analytical concepts taught in the course. Generative AI use during in-class quizzes or activities may be permitted or prohibited depending on the learning objective of the activity; instructions will be provided when necessary.

Finally, do not upload confidential, private, proprietary, or otherwise restricted data or materials to generative AI systems. You are responsible for ensuring that your use of these tools complies with course policies, University policies, and applicable requirements regarding academic integrity, privacy, and data use.
